## Context

Financial reconciliation with transportation providers was resolved weeks after each trip, generating losses and overwhelming support.

## My role

I was the designer in charge of taking the project end to end — research, information architecture, UI, and prototyping — supported by a senior expert on the team. I aligned functional definitions with product, researched how operational teams worked on both sides, and carried the solution from exploration through launch support, working closely with product, business, and engineering.

## How I worked

In interviews we found that reviewing a weeks-old trip meant reconstructing from memory something that should have been logged the same day. That finding shifted the focus of the problem: it wasn't about making the end-of-process review faster, but about moving it into the day-to-day.

<ProductDivider eyebrow="Research" label="Reviewing a weeks-old trip meant reconstructing from memory something that should have been logged the same day." />

Between business, tech, and design, we shaped the logic that let the system validate services without relying on manual, case-by-case auditing. My part was to make that definition tangible: I designed the progressive-validation mechanism, tailored to carriers' real way of working, and iterated the proposal with the team until we considered it ready for testing. I also helped draw the line between what the system could resolve on its own and what had to stay with business and finance — that boundary mattered as much as the functionality itself.

When data volume made it impossible to audit trips one by one on screen, I proposed an executive panel with filtering and parameterized downloads instead of forcing a row-by-row review.

## Result

Fewer unrecoverable discrepancies, disputes resolved in hours instead of weeks, and coordinators who stopped reconstructing cases from memory. The lesson: in complex B2B tools, interface design only works if it's paired with how the team structures incentives outside the screen.
