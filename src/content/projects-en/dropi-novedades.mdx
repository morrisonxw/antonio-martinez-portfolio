*This is a design challenge set by Dropi, not a production project. Everything shown on screen (buyers, orders, deadlines, carriers, and outcomes) is a synthetic scenario I built to design and review the experience. There is no real data, no interviews with sellers, and no connection to carriers. The prototype interface is in Spanish; labels are translated in the text.*

## The business challenge

Dropi connects sellers, buyers, and carriers. When a delivery fails, the carrier reports an **exception**, and each one words it differently: "nobody was there," "address doesn't exist," "the buyer refused it." There are dozens of statuses that often mean the same thing, and the dropshipper is left with a technical or incomplete message and no clear next step, often writing to support.

The challenge was to design an experience that helps **increase successful deliveries**, with one explicit warning: today most exceptions show up as "resolved," yet few end up delivered, because "resolving" almost always means returning the package. Measuring success with that label is the metric trap.

## Goals

### Business goals

- Increase the successful delivery rate, without counting returns as recovered deliveries.
- Reduce dependence on support to handle each exception.

### Product goals

- Turn heterogeneous reports into a clear decision that stays consistent across carriers.
- Separate management progress from the shipment outcome.
- Lay a foundation that scales toward priorities, assignment, and automation.

### User goals

- Understand what happened without decoding shipping codes.
- Know what they can do now, and what depends on someone else.
- Know how the order ended: delivered or returned.

## My contribution

I took the project end to end: strategic framing, desk research, archetypes and journeys, ideation with behavioral design, a mid-to-high-fidelity prototype, UX writing, and QA. I worked with AI assistance to organize, explore, write, and implement the prototype; the product decisions and editorial review were mine.

## The dropshipper is not a logistics specialist

One framing decision changed the rest of the project: not treating the dropshipper as an expert in shipping. Their job is to sell and earn a profit. Logistics is a condition for that sale to close, not the reason they come to Dropi.

So the problem was not framed as "teach them to read carrier statuses," but as an interruption to their main work that demands a quick decision. The job that guided the design:

> **When a delivery has a problem, I want to know quickly what I can do to protect the sale, so I don't waste time decoding logistics processes or depend on support for every case.**

This has a practical consequence: the interface doesn't teach transport concepts unless they are essential to decide. It turns complexity into a recommendation, explains only what is needed, and ties each action to the progress of the sale.

## What we learned

The discovery was **desk research**: I reviewed public sources from Colombian carriers and two shipping-platform references. It does not replace user interviews, and I treated it that way, separating sources, inferences, assumptions, and open questions.

The most important finding is that different barriers hide under the same exception: sometimes the problem is understanding the report; other times information is missing, contradictory, or the action depends on the carrier. Translating codes was not enough: the design had to show who can do what and what is still pending.

To avoid designing only for the easy case, I built three synthetic archetypes that represent work contexts, not confirmed segments or user percentages:

- Someone handling **one specific exception** who must decide about a single order.
- Someone **splitting attention across many cases** who needs to choose which to review first.
- Someone who finds **contradictory information** and needs to know which fact can support an action.

## Product decisions

### 1. Translate the report into a decision

The actionable items are not carrier statuses in simpler words: they are Dropi's own interpretation. The proposal adds an intelligence layer that cross-references the report with order and buyer information, management history, similar resolutions, and other available signals, and groups reports by the decision the seller needs to make, not by each carrier's code.

AI is framed as an interpretation and recommendation capability, not an unquestionable authority. When information is insufficient or contradictory, the experience must show the uncertainty, ask for confirmation, or hand the case off. In the prototype this layer is represented by static scenarios and data; there is no connection to a real engine.

### 2. Tell action, support, and waiting apart

"Waiting for the buyer" or "waiting for the carrier" are not actionable: they are dependencies that explain why a case is still open. The interface can report them, but it doesn't turn them into the seller's next step. An actionable item expresses a decision the seller can make that moves the shipment forward.

For "nobody was there to receive," contacting the buyer may be necessary to agree on a date, but it is a means, not the outcome. The main step is to **schedule the second delivery**, and it doesn't force the seller to log a conversation that may have happened by phone or WhatsApp.

### 3. A dedicated tool for exceptions

Exceptions live in their own panel within sales management, not as one more card in general monitoring. Monitoring helps understand how the business is doing; this tool helps intervene on orders with a specific delivery problem. Mixing them would force the seller to filter out commercial metrics right when they need to decide, and the separation leaves room to grow toward priorities, assignment, and automation without turning the sales dashboard into an exceptions inbox.

### 4. Managed doesn't mean delivered

After an action, the case is not closed. It moves to **Managed** and states which outcome is still to be confirmed. Only proof of delivery or return moves it to **Closed**, where the two outcomes are kept apart.

### 5. What I decided to remove

I also removed what didn't help resolve the exception: monetary values, recovered-money indicators, mandatory forms, success promises, and bulk-management controls. Bulk management stays as a future opportunity, but first I had to check that the basic unit (one case, one decision, one action, and one follow-up) was clear. Two exceptions can share a label and still require different decisions when the buyer, history, or carrier changes.

## The solution

The screen organizes work by management status. In the detail view, the right panel opens with a highlighted card: the time left, the situation as understood by Dropi, a short explanation, and the suggested action. The alternative action, such as **Cancel delivery**, lives in the split button's menu. The buyer data, shipment details, the source report, and the history stay on secondary levels: they aren't removed, they are ordered by the decision.

### UX writing: say only what is known

The rule was simple: the user should be able to anticipate what will happen when they activate a control without having to interpret it. Verbs that describe the decision moving the case forward were preferred, and hypotheses were never presented as facts.

The work followed a sequence in which every stage made explicit what was evidence and what was construction:

1. **Strategic framing.** I set the north star before deciding on any interface: increase successful deliveries, with the dropshipper as the main user. I wrote down the questions that could change the solution: what the seller can actually do, which exceptions are recoverable, and what "resolved" means today.
2. **Desk research and archetypes.** Sources, contrasts between carriers, and three synthetic usage contexts so I wouldn't design only for the simplest case.
3. **Ideation with behavioral design.** I treated it as a design hypothesis, not a claim about user psychology: few recognizable actions, urgency only when a concrete condition exists, and seller control when an action has consequences. The chosen concept was **few actions, no decoding exceptions**.
4. **Mid-to-high-fidelity prototype.** It turned the definition into a navigable experience to review statuses and journeys for contact, rescheduling, inquiry, return, and closure.
5. **Visual and functional QA.** It fixed the detail hierarchy, CTA consistency, button accessibility, and the difference between an executed action and a confirmed outcome.

There are three design rules for this evolution: automation must be **granular** (by exception type, action, carrier, or confidence level), **traceable** (it reports what it understood, what it did, and which outcome is still pending), and **reversible** when possible. And it never closes an exception just because it ran an action. It's also best not to treat "conservative" or "enthusiast" as fixed labels for people: not everyone wants to automate everything.

### What still needs checking

The prototype doesn't integrate with carriers, doesn't contact buyers, and doesn't run a real AI engine. The next step is a moderated usability test with dropshippers to check:

- Whether they understand the action without decoding the report.
- Whether they tell waiting apart from an outcome.
- Whether the Pending, Managed, and Closed hierarchy matches how they work.

It's also necessary to verify which actions and events actually exist in Dropi and at each carrier. After that would come an operational definition of the beta: comparing orders with exceptions handled through the experience against an agreed reference, without counting returns as recovered deliveries and without promising an improvement figure before there is a baseline.

## Lessons learned

The most important decision wasn't designing a tidier inbox, but changing the unit of the experience: from a technical status the seller has to decode to a decision they can recognize, execute, and follow until the outcome is known.

The design keeps uncertainty visible. When only a message was sent, the case stays open; when a reply is missing, the screen says so; when the order came back, it shows as canceled and not as a successful management. That separation protects the user's understanding and avoids measuring success with an administrative label.

And I learned to keep the scope honest: a prototype ready to be tested is not proof of impact. Documenting what is evidence and what is construction made the work more defensible.
